Language Acuqirer's Handbook Appendix
=
Through years of study of a half-dozen languages, I've had the opportunity
to try out all kinds of acquisition methods. What I've learned is that
results come as a consequence of the *way* that the language is interacted
with. Analytical methods produce stilted and incorrect speech. Readers
fail to learn unwritten aspects of intonation in speech, and struggle
with listening comprehension. Methods not focusing on literacy produce
the ability to do well in a variety of simple situations, but as soon
as complex ideas of the kind that interest adults arise, it can be
a challenge due to having about 1/4th the vocabulary of a reader.[^1]
A variety of exposure, especially to progressively more complex language
material is the answer to this vocabulary problem.

Phase 1: Basic acquisition, Phonemic awareness, and common vocabulary
===
[comment]: avoid we. avoid "the brain". avoid exclamations. avoid "let there be no mistake"
Most learners attempt to use a method that skips this basic phase,
like opening a traditional textbook. We can't remember all this random,
meaningless information in our native languages. Let there be no mistake:
this is the most time-consuming part of language acquisition. It takes
thousands of hours! Thankfully, studies of early-life language acquisition
have shed light on what happens here. Through massive exposure to the
language, without understanding it at all, the brain builds an intuition
for what sounds are contained in the language, what sounds come together
or don't, what words and word-boundaries sound like, and eventually even
a small number of simple words such as interesting everyday lexemes like
"hot", "fun", or "toy".

We can learn this stuff for a new language easily, using the same
mechanism the babies use. This is a very limited domain -- most language
shave just a few dozen phonemes, a small inventory of sentence-level
intonation patterns, and a few curveballs like hard-to-pronounce
consonant clusters, phonemic tones or stress, a rhotic sound that kids
can't pronounce, etc.

Even an hour of natural speech in a high-quality recording will most
likely contain every pieces of such constructive elements. All of these
methods are likely to work, and are advisable as the _first_ thing to
do for language acquisition, before there has been a chance to learn
them wrong through reading or thinking analytically about the language's
content:

Phonemic Acquisition Strategies
==
- Listening to the same thing over and over.
- Enjoying music.
- Exposure to a large volume of audio-visual media without the written word.
- Exposure to a lot of audio material like podcasts, without understanding much.
- Being in the presene of a group of natives conversing with each other.

As will become a theme, the 2nd language learner will never be able
to do the exact thing the theory suggests, due to previous knowledge,
time constraints, or an unwillingness to do it. When listening to this
material, we will quickly identify and misinterpret things in the way
babies can't. We know names of people and places, international loanwords,
the context that the material is discussing, and more. We can't help
but start understanding this information, to the detriment of pure
context-free acquisition. When an adult learner of a language says a word
that also exists in his native language, or that he learned deliberately
and so on, that is where he is most likely to fumble the native accent
when uttering it.

Thankfully these elements, while substantial enough to flag a non-native
learner, are not substantial enough to have any other effects. I believe
this is the only reason that adult-learned native language acquisition
is not possible.

Phase 2: Narrow-domain ability
===
[comment]: "kids" to "children"?
Kids can only talk coherently about toys, games, food, dogs, and other
_concrete_ things. Adults want to discuss philosophy, football, politics,
literature, professional life, and other _abstract_ things. Here we draw
a distinction between language acquisition (kids do this) and advanced
literacy and language learning (required to be a functional adult).

Timed language study approaches
==
Long sessions (eg. 4hrs)
===
Ideal for talking to people, listening to something, watching something,
or reading a book. This is the best thing to do, but the downside is that
it is naturally limited to only once or twice per day. When learning
more than one language, another method must be employed, maybe in addition
to doing this.
AJATT (almost 16 hours/day)
===
this method has the same problem as long sessions, in that we have to
limit ourselves to must one language. All Japanese All The Time is a
famous blog that advocates just that. Make everything Japanese. Unlinke
the long session time management strategy, what the specific activity
is becomes irrelevant -- the AJATTer does all his daily activities in
the target language, to the extent possible. In fact, the original method
suggests using an earbud at all time, to the distraction of everything
else, to get "passive input". For the polyglot student, there is
some value in trying this out. I call it ALATT: All Languages All The
Time. For this, we setup our mobile device to have a large quantity of
audio material[^2] which is very easy[^3] to continue listening to. It is
also helpful to ensure access to films and books in the target languages
to ALATT those activities too. Some people report success using social
media accounts to get algorithmic content served up. I prefer to move
away from those platforms; beyond the obvious problem from using them,
the polyglot student, more than anyone else, needs _control_ over the
study content[^4]. There are a lot of reasons for this[^5].
A count-up timer, ie. "tracking your time"
===
The appeal of this approach is that when tracking time this way the
tracking never leads to instructing yourself to stop engaging with the
target language material. The student just continues studying for as
long as desired, Maybe to completion of the media or some stopping place,
and enjoys the benefit of knowing how much time has been spent doing
what. Again, this method breaks down when studying more than a few
languages, because it is just not natural to engage with them all
every day. After about five languages, the issue starts to become how
to remember to spend enough time in each of them to achieve targets. One
place where I find count-up time tracking useful is when it is all
automated, and I can see these stats as a kind of guideline to whether
or not enough time is being spent and the language diversity target is
being met. I think for less than about five languages, tracking is
a very powerful tool that can realistically be enough on its own to
ensure acquisition.

The best tool one can hope for is nothing more than a simple timer. I
prefer setting it to a fourteen-minute countdown and cycling thorugh my
languages in the morning. I've tried various things -- a phone timer,
a stopwatch. What I've found is that the found is too distracting -- I

worked -- I studied a lot. The phone has apps for this too -- but again,
too distracting. With around 5 languages, this free use of time is an
issue. I can't study them as long as I want and expect to improve them
all each day. That's why I've opted for the count-down watch. At first I
had a casio F-91w. It can't count down, it just has a count-up stopwatch
and an alarm that can be set for a specific time. It became very clear
that this wasn't going to work. The main issue I've found with this many
languages is that it takes too long in-between switching languages. It
is too easy to go do something else, procrastinate, or waste time looking

Casio. It has the necessary count-down timer, and even has a mode for
automatically restarting the timer countdown at zero. It also happens
to be a more stylish black watch with better water resistance, and is
the same price -- $18. I find that this works best, and I time everything
with the fourteen-minute timer I set -- morning language study, resistance
training, anki time, meditation time. It's the perfect time -- a
schedulistically insignificant time.

The count-down alarm is the pentalgot's[^6] best friend. Once a system
has been decided on for how much to study, there is no ability to waffle
around and miss targets. Following advice I've heard from both medieval
and modern teachers of Latin, the best way to do this is to have a
time when it is done everyday, and to not infringe upon that time under
any pretense. The best time is probably first thing in the morning,
because that is when the realities of the day haven't had the opportunity
to impose themselves on the reading time. For the bilingual student,
reading before bed is also a fine habit. I would rather avoid the question
of "how long", which gets at the core problem of this tactic. Fifteen
minutes is enough to read about ten books per year, even.
