14 ## The Four-Level Editing System
15 
16 ### Overview
17 
18 Professional editors work through four distinct levels, never mixing concerns:
19 
20 | Level | Focus | Time Investment |
21 |-------|-------|-----------------|
22 | 1. Developmental | Structure, content, argument | 30-50% |
23 | 2. Line | Style, clarity, flow | 25-35% |
24 | 3. Copy | Grammar, consistency, facts | 20-30% |
25 | 4. Proofreading | Typos, formatting | 10-15% |
26 
27 **Critical principle**: Work through levels sequentially. Fixing typos before addressing structure wastes time.
28 
29 ### Level 1: Developmental Editing
30 
31 **Focus**: Big-picture organization, narrative flow, argument structure
32 
33 **Questions to ask:**
34 - What is the core message? (Write it in one sentence)
35 - Does every section advance this message?
36 - Is information in logical order?
37 - Are there gaps in logic or evidence?
38 
39 **Technique**: Reverse outline
40 - After drafting, outline what you wrote
41 - Each paragraph = one bullet
42 - Gaps and redundancies become visible
43 
44 ### Level 2: Line Editing
45 
46 **Focus**: Sentence-level improvements, paragraph flow, voice consistency
47 
48 **Questions to ask:**
49 - Is this the clearest way to say this?
50 - Can I say this in fewer words?
51 - Does each paragraph have one clear focus?
52 - Are transitions smooth?
53 
54 **Technique**: Read aloud
55 - Unclear passages trip your tongue
56 - Run-on sentences make you gasp for breath
57 - Awkward phrasing becomes obvious
58 
59 ### Level 3: Copyediting
60 
61 **Focus**: Grammar, punctuation, style guide adherence, fact-checking
62 
63 **Questions to ask:**
64 - Is this grammatically correct?
65 - Is it consistent with prior usage?
66 - Are facts and citations accurate?
67 
68 **Technique**: Separate passes for different concerns
69 - One pass for punctuation
70 - One pass for consistency
71 - One pass for facts
72 
73 ### Level 4: Proofreading
74 
75 **Focus**: Typos, formatting, layout issues
76 
77 **Questions to ask:**
78 - Are there any remaining errors?
79 - Is formatting consistent?
80 - Do all links work?
81 
82 **Technique**: Read backwards
83 - Forces focus on individual words
84 - Prevents brain from "filling in" expected text
85 - Catches typos the eye normally skips

87 ## Revision Strategy Research
88 
89 ### Graham & Perin Meta-Analysis (2007)
90 
91 Analysis of 123 studies with 154 effect sizes:
92 
93 | Intervention | Effect Size |
94 |--------------|-------------|
95 | Strategy instruction | 0.82 (highest) |
96 | Sentence combining | 0.50 |
97 | Process writing | 0.32 |
98 | Grammar instruction | -0.32 (negative) |
99 
100 **Key insight**: Teaching explicit revision strategies has nearly triple the impact of teaching grammar rules.
101 
102 ### What "Strategy Instruction" Means
103 
104 The most effective approach combines:
105 1. **Cognitive strategies**: Planning, organizing, self-regulation
106 2. **Metacognitive strategies**: Self-assessment, reflection
107 3. **Procedural knowledge**: Specific techniques to apply
108 
109 **Implication**: Having a revision process matters more than knowing grammar rules.
110 
111 ### Timing Research
112 
113 Studies show separating drafting from editing improves quality:
114 - Self-assessment before and after revision improves both self-efficacy and writing
115 - 24-hour gap between drafting and editing recommended (cognitive refresh)
116 - "Kill your darlings" easier with time distance

118 ## The Conciseness Pass
119 
120 ### Scott Hotaling's Framework (Limnology and Oceanography Letters, 2020)
121 
122 Three types of filler that reduce conciseness:
123 
124 **1. Qualifiers (adverbs)**
125 - Examples: quickly, extremely, frequently, very, really
126 - Impact: Often add zero information
127 - Fix: Remove entirely in most cases
128 
129 **2. Prepositional phrase chains**
130 - Examples: "in this case," "among other things," "on the other hand"
131 - Impact: Make sentences jumbled and unclear
132 - Fix: Replace multi-word phrases with single words
133 
134 **3. Overused transitions**
135 - Examples: however, meanwhile, thus, furthermore
136 - Impact: Create verbal clutter when overused
137 - Fix: Remove extraneous transitions, combine sentences

155

157 |----------|-----------|

186 ## Active Voice Conversion
187 
188 ### Identification
189 
190 Passive voice pattern: form of "to be" + past participle
191 
192 | Passive Indicator | Example |
193 |-------------------|---------|
194 | is/was + past participle | "was written" |
195 | are/were + past participle | "were found" |
196 | has/have been + past participle | "has been completed" |
197 | will be + past participle | "will be reviewed" |
198 
199 ### Conversion Process
200 
201 1. Identify the true actor (who/what performs the action)
202 2. Make the actor the subject
203 3. Use active verb form
204 
205 **Examples:**
206 
207 | Passive | Active |
208 |---------|--------|
209 | The report was written by the team | The team wrote the report |
210 | Errors were found in the code | We found errors in the code |
211 | The meeting was scheduled for 3pm | I scheduled the meeting for 3pm |
212 | A decision was made to proceed | The board decided to proceed |
213 | The feature has been implemented | We implemented the feature |
214 | Concerns were raised about costs | Stakeholders raised concerns about costs |
215 
216 ### When to Keep Passive
217 
218 Keep passive voice only when:
219 - **Actor unknown**: "The window was broken" (no one knows by whom)
220 - **Actor irrelevant**: "The samples were collected" (focus on samples, not collector)
221 - **Object emphasis**: "Three bugs were fixed" (emphasis on bugs)
222 - **Hedging appropriate**: "Mistakes were made" (deliberately vague)
223 
224 **Default**: Convert to active unless specific reason to keep passive.

229

231 - Average comprehension: 61%
232 - Sentence length: 28 words average
233 
234 **Revised** (plain language):
235 - Average comprehension: 81% (+33%)
236 - Sentence length: 16 words average
237 - Length: 40% shorter
238

240 - Sentence length reduced from 28 to 16 words
241 - Legal jargon removed: "hereinafter," "aforementioned," "pursuant to"
242 - Active voice throughout
243 - Clear headings and white space

296 ## Self-Editing Techniques
297 
298 ### Technique 1: The "So What?" Test
299 
300 **Application**: After each paragraph, ask "So what?"
301 
302 If you can't answer clearly, the paragraph needs revision.
303 
304 **Example:**
305 > "The system uses a microservices architecture."
306 
307 So what?
308 
309 > "The system uses a microservices architecture, so you can update individual components without redeploying everything."
310 
311 ### Technique 2: One Idea Per Sentence
312 
313 **Application**: If sentence contains "and," "but," or "however," consider splitting.
314 
315 **Before:**
316 > "The API is fast and reliable but requires authentication and uses rate limiting."
317 
318 **After:**
319 > "The API is fast and reliable. It requires authentication and uses rate limiting."
320 
321 ### Technique 3: Front-Load Subject and Verb
322 
323 **Application**: First 7-8 words should contain subject and main verb.
324 
325 **Before:**
326 > "In order to understand the implications of the proposed changes to the system architecture, developers need to review the documentation."
327 
328 **After:**
329 > "Developers need to review the documentation to understand the proposed changes."
330 
331 ### Technique 4: The Grandmother Test
332 
333 **Application**: Would a non-expert understand the main point?
334 
335 If not, simplify until they would.
336 
337 ### Technique 5: The Cut Test
338 
339 **Application**: For each sentence, ask "Can I remove this without losing meaning?"
340 
341 If yes, remove it.
342 
343 ### Technique 6: Kill Your Darlings
344 
345 **Application**: Identify your favorite phrases. Evaluate them critically.
346 
347 Writers overvalue their clever phrasing (overconfidence bias). Favorite phrases often serve the writer's ego, not the reader's understanding.
348 
349 ### Technique 7: Convert Nominalizations
350 
351 **Application**: Change noun phrases back to verbs.
352 
353 | Nominalization | Verb Form |
354 |----------------|-----------|
355 | made an investigation into | investigated |
356 | reached a conclusion about | concluded |
357 | gave consideration to | considered |
358 | performed an analysis of | analyzed |
359 | made a decision to | decided to |
360 
361 ### Technique 8: The 24-Hour Rule
362 
363 **Application**: Wait 24 hours between drafting and editing.
364 
365 Cognitive refresh reveals:
366 - Unclear passages you thought were clear
367 - Redundancies you missed
368 - Missing transitions you assumed were there

370 ## Common Revision Mistakes
371 
372 ### Mistake 1: Editing While Drafting
373 
374 **Problem**: Interrupts creative flow, wastes time on text you'll delete
375 **Fix**: Draft first, edit later (separate sessions)
376 
377 ### Mistake 2: Starting with Grammar
378 
379 **Problem**: You'll fix sentences you later delete
380 **Fix**: Work developmental → line → copy → proof
381 
382 ### Mistake 3: Over-Reliance on Tools
383 
384 **Problem**: Tools don't understand context or meaning
385 **Fix**: Use tools for detection, not decision-making
386 
387 ### Mistake 4: Accepting All Suggestions
388 
389 **Problem**: Not every "issue" is actually a problem
390 **Fix**: Evaluate each suggestion against context and purpose
391 
392 ### Mistake 5: Ignoring Reader Feedback
393 
394 **Problem**: Authors can't see their own blind spots
395 **Fix**: Seek external feedback; it correlates with improvement
396 
397 ### Mistake 6: Single-Pass Revision
398 
399 **Problem**: Different issues require different attention
400 **Fix**: Multiple passes, each with focused purpose
