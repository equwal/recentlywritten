34 <span class="a">+Abstract</span>
35 <span class="a">+The proliferation of generative AI systems has created new challenges for the Free and Open Source</span>
36 <span class="a">+Software (FOSS) community, particularly regarding how traditional copyleft principles should apply</span>
37 <span class="a">+when open source code is used to train AI models. This article introduces the Contextual Copyleft AI</span>
38 <span class="a">+(CCAI) license, a novel licensing mechanism that extends copyleft requirements from training data to</span>
39 <span class="a">+the resulting generative AI models. The CCAI license offers significant advantages, including</span>
40 <span class="a">+enhanced developer control, incentivization of open source AI development, and mitigation of open-</span>
41 <span class="a">+washing practices. This is demonstrated through a structured three-part evaluation framework that</span>
42 <span class="a">+examines (1) legal feasibility under current copyright law, (2) policy justification comparing traditional</span>
43 <span class="a">+software and AI contexts, and (3) synthesis of cross-contextual benefits and risks. However, the</span>
44 <span class="a">+increased risk profile of open source AI, particularly the potential for direct misuse, necessitates</span>
45 <span class="a">+complementary regulatory approaches to achieve an appropriate risk-benefit balance. The paper</span>
46 <span class="a">+concludes that when implemented within a robust regulatory environment focused on responsible AI</span>
47 <span class="a">+usage, the CCAI license provides a viable mechanism for preserving and adapting core FOSS</span>
48 <span class="a">+principles to the evolving landscape of generative AI development.</span>

55 1. Introduction
56 <span class="a">+With the recent increase in the number (Gozalo-Brizuela and Garrido-Merchan 2023) and power</span>
57 <span class="a">+(Bandi, Adapa, and Kuchi 2023) of generative AI systems, the open source community faces new</span>
58 <span class="a">+challenges in responding effectively. There is considerable discussion regarding the adoption of open</span>
59 <span class="a">+source generative AI models, particularly given the substantial opportunities and risks that accompany</span>
60 <span class="a">+making these systems open (Eiras, Petrov, Vidgen, Schroeder, et al. 2024; Floridi et al. 2025). Some</span>
61 <span class="a">+participants in the debate suggest shifting the focus away from open source generative AI, emphasizing</span>
62 <span class="a">+the need for responsible AI through behavioral use licensing instead (Contractor et al. 2022).</span>
63 <span class="a">+Questions persist about how the open source community should manage models trained on open</span>
64 <span class="a">+source code (a practice notably illustrated by GitHub’s CoPilot, as discussed in Section 4.2). This</span>
65 <span class="a">+dialogue focuses on preserving and adapting the rules, principles, and practices of the Free and Open</span>
66 <span class="a">+Source Software (FOSS) movement to navigate the evolving technical landscape introduced by</span>
67 <span class="a">+generative AI.</span>
68 <span class="a">+</span>
69 <span class="a">+         One way the FOSS movement has historically protected free software is through “copyleft”</span>
70 <span class="a">+licenses. These licenses require that derivative works be distributed under the same open source license</span>
71 <span class="a">+as the original work. This safeguards free and open source software because copyleft propagates</span>
72 <span class="a">+licenses from source material to derivative works, creating a viral effect where both original and</span>
73 <span class="a">+derivative works remain free and open (Phillips 2007). If attributes such as “freedom” and “openness”</span>
74 <span class="a">+are desirable for generative AI systems, then copyleft may provide a promising mechanism for the</span>
75 <span class="a">+FOSS community to uphold traditional software freedoms and extend their normative commitments</span>
76 <span class="a">+into the emerging field of generative AI.</span>
77 <span class="a">+</span>
78 <span class="a">+       This paper examines whether such an extension of copyleft principles is desirable and explores</span>
79 <span class="a">+potential mechanisms for achieving it. The Contextual Copyleft AI (CCAI) license is proposed, which may</span>
80 <span class="a">+cover both traditional software and generative AI contexts. Then, the challenges of evaluating licenses</span>
81 <span class="a">+that cross contextual boundaries are discussed, considering the legal and policy considerations in each</span>
82 <span class="a">+context. This leads to an argument about how such a license should be evaluated. Next, this structured</span>
83 <span class="a">+evaluation is completed for the CCAI license. The analysis concludes that the license could be</span>
84 <span class="a">+beneficial, particularly when it is used within a strong regulatory environment. The license provides</span>
85 <span class="a">+developers with greater control over how their code is used, encourages the development of open</span>
86 <span class="a">+source generative AI systems, and addresses concerns related to openwashing. Regulating the</span>
87 <span class="a">+responsible use of AI would address the risk-benefit imbalance inherent in copyleft, as highlighted by</span>
88 <span class="a">+the evaluation. This license highlights emerging cases where carefully constructed and thoughtfully</span>
89 <span class="a">+</span>

91 <span class="a">+</span>
92 evaluated copyleft licenses cross contextual boundaries, offering essential insights into how to preserve
93 <span class="a">+and adapt free and open source norms, principles, and practices in a changing technological landscape.</span>

95 <span class="a">+2. The Contextual Copyleft AI (CCAI) License</span>
96 <span class="a">+This section proposes a new open source license that harnesses the features of copyleft to enable</span>
97 <span class="a">+developers to allow only open source generative AI models to train on their code. The copyleft scheme</span>
98 <span class="a">+enables copyright holders to permit users of their software to use, modify, and redistribute their code</span>
99 <span class="a">+in any way, provided that the modified or redistributed version is licensed under the same terms as</span>
100 <span class="a">+the original work (Stallman 2002). Fundamentally, copyleft is a mechanism that leverages the existing</span>
101 <span class="a">+copyright system to protect and promote the freedom of users to run, copy, distribute, study, change,</span>
102 <span class="a">+and improve software.</span>
103 <span class="a">+</span>
104 <span class="a">+       We will refer to the proposed license as the Contextual Copyleft AI (CCAI) license, a name that</span>
105 <span class="a">+draws on this license’s ability to cross technical and legal contexts. This ability necessitates a nuanced</span>
106 <span class="a">+evaluation of this license in the sections that follow. The full license text and a clause that can be added</span>
107 <span class="a">+to existing open source licenses are included in the appendix of this paper. The CCAI license consists</span>
108 <span class="a">+of three main components:</span>
109 <span class="a">+</span>
110 <span class="a">+    1. Software freedom: Users can run the software, modify the code to ﬁt their needs, and</span>
111 <span class="a">+         distribute copies of the software.</span>
112 <span class="a">+</span>
113 <span class="a">+    2. Copyleft requirement: Any verbatim copy, modiﬁed version, derivative work, or model trained</span>
114 <span class="a">+         on this code must also be wholly licensed under CCAI with no additional restrictions. This</span>
115 <span class="a">+         includes a description of the training data, the code used to train and run the model, and the</span>
116 <span class="a">+         model’s parameters for generative AI models.1</span>
117 <span class="a">+</span>
118 <span class="a">+    3. Distribution clariﬁcation: Any distributed CCAI software must include source code or provide</span>
119 <span class="a">+         a way to access it. Providing access to software over the network is considered equivalent to</span>
120 <span class="a">+         distributing the software.</span>
121 <span class="a">+</span>
122 <span class="a">+The CCAI license incorporates a copyleft, share-alike clause to propagate open source requirements</span>
123 <span class="a">+from training data to the AI model itself. It adheres to the open source norm of imposing terms on</span>
124 <span class="a">+distribution without using code to protect software freedom via an extended definition of “distribute.”</span>
125 <span class="a">+Furthermore, it enhances existing open source copyleft licenses by explicitly defining terms related to</span>

129 <span class="a">+the model, where the data originated, how it was selected and obtained, and discussion of any training or ﬂiting methods.</span>

176 <span class="a">+3. Evaluating Licenses Propagating Between Contexts</span>
177 <span class="a">+Licenses that are designed to propagate between works in different technical and legal contexts pose</span>
178 <span class="a">+unique challenges in evaluating their legal and policy implications. This section argues that the</span>
179 <span class="a">+changing context of such licenses necessitates three components. First, a legal analysis is conducted</span>
180 <span class="a">+to determine whether the propagation mechanism is permissible within the existing legal framework.</span>
181 <span class="a">+Second, a policy analysis is performed to assess the individual benefits of copyleft within each context.</span>
182 <span class="a">+This analysis must then be synthesized to determine the overall desirability of such licenses. While the</span>
183 <span class="a">+necessity of the legal analysis is straightforward, the policy consideration is more nuanced. An open</span>
184 <span class="a">+source license may be desirable in its original context and be able to propagate to another context, but</span>
185 <span class="a">+the license may not be desirable in that new context. There are additional considerations, as the</span>
186 <span class="a">+primary justification for copyleft licenses is necessarily context-dependent; therefore, the rationale for</span>
187 <span class="a">+a license will change as it propagates between contexts. After introducing this framework for</span>
188 <span class="a">+evaluating licenses that propagate across different technical and legal contexts, it will be applied to the</span>
189 <span class="a">+CCAI license proposed above.</span>
190 <span class="a">+</span>
191 <span class="a">+         Technical contexts refer to sets of software that encompass work with similar risks of being</span>
192 <span class="a">+made open source. For instance, traditional software refers to software that is commonly open sourced</span>
193 <span class="a">+today, including desktop applications, computer science tools, and server software. The risks</span>
194 <span class="a">+associated with traditional open source software may include security vulnerabilities, dependency</span>
195 <span class="a">+management challenges, and concerns regarding project sustainability (Zajdel, Costa, and Mili, 2022).</span>
196 <span class="a">+Another context could be generative AI because the risks of releasing a generative AI model as open</span>
197 <span class="a">+source would be fundamentally different; the risks are focused on the harm that could be caused either</span>
198 <span class="a">+by humans using the model or by a misaligned model itself (Amodei et al. 2016). These technical</span>
199 <span class="a">+contexts may or may not exist within the same legal contexts. Typically, technologies operating in</span>
200 <span class="a">+various technical contexts are governed by the same overarching legal framework, although specific</span>
201 <span class="a">+regulatory requirements may differ.</span>
202 <span class="a">+</span>
203 <span class="a">+         The first component in evaluating a license designed to propagate between works in different</span>
204 <span class="a">+contexts is a legal evaluation of the feasibility of the propagation mechanism. While the necessity of</span>
205 <span class="a">+such analysis is self-evident, the examination itself may prove complex. This evaluation must primarily</span>
206 <span class="a">+</span>
207 <span class="a">+                                                                                                                    5</span>
208 <span class="a">+</span>
209 establish two elements: first, that the work to which the license propagates qualifies as a derivative of
210 <span class="a">+the original work under applicable copyright law; and second, that the incorporation of the original</span>
211 <span class="a">+work within the derivative work falls outside the scope of the fair use doctrine. If these conditions are</span>
212 <span class="a">+satisfied, it can generally be concluded that the license governing the original work may impose legal</span>
213 <span class="a">+obligations on its use within the derivative work, including the enforcement of share-alike provisions.</span>
214 <span class="a">+</span>
215 <span class="a">+         The second component is a policy evaluation of the use of copyleft in each context</span>
216 <span class="a">+individually. This must be done in each context individually because different technical contexts</span>
217 <span class="a">+present fundamentally different risk profiles that affect the cost-benefit analysis underlying the</span>
218 <span class="a">+justification of free software. These varying risks mean that the same copyleft license may be justified</span>
219 <span class="a">+in one context but unjustified in another, even when applied to functionally related software. The</span>
220 <span class="a">+conclusions from the separate policy evaluations must be considered jointly in determining whether</span>
221 <span class="a">+such a license should be used.</span>
222 <span class="a">+</span>
223 <span class="a">+         Copyleft licenses are politically justified by their ability to protect and promote free and open</span>
224 <span class="a">+source software.4 Thus, the justification for using copyleft within a specific context depends on the</span>
225 <span class="a">+rationale for using free and open source software in that context. If the use of free software generally</span>
226 <span class="a">+can’t be shown to be beneficial in a technical and legal context, then copyleft can’t be shown to be</span>
227 <span class="a">+beneficial either. Furthermore, the justification of free software must also consider the risks associated</span>
228 <span class="a">+with making a piece of software open source. Thus, when technical contexts change, the specifics of</span>
229 <span class="a">+the justification for free software, and therefore the justification for using copyleft, must change as</span>
230 <span class="a">+well. The overarching argument in support of free software may remain unchanged; however, the</span>
231 <span class="a">+specifics of the argument must evolve either way. For instance, copyleft on a traditional piece of</span>
232 <span class="a">+software may be justified by a pragmatic argument regarding the quality of software produced by free</span>
233 <span class="a">+and open source communities. If this license propagates to a generative AI system, the justification</span>
234 <span class="a">+may or may not change, remaining pragmatic or taking on moral terms instead. However, the specifics</span>
235 <span class="a">+of the argument must change due to the different fundamental risks associated with making the</span>
236 <span class="a">+software open source. Therefore, the policy evaluation must be conducted individually for each</span>
237 <span class="a">+technical context.</span>
238 <span class="a">+</span>
239 <span class="a">+         The third component is a policy analysis of copyleft propagation as a whole. While the policy</span>
240 <span class="a">+analysis justifying the use of copyleft and open source must be conducted individually in each context,</span>
241 <span class="a">+the results should be considered collectively to make a final determination as to whether the license</span>
242 <span class="a">+</span>

244 <span class="a">+</span>
245 <span class="a">+                                                                                                                    6</span>
246 <span class="a">+</span>
247 should be used or not. This synthesis must evaluate the specific benefits and drawbacks of the
248 <span class="a">+propagation mechanism itself, including: whether it empowers developers by giving them appropriate</span>
249 <span class="a">+control over how their work is used across contexts; whether it creates positive incentives for open</span>
250 <span class="a">+source development in the new context; whether it helps maintain the integrity of open source</span>
251 <span class="a">+principles as they extend to new technologies; and whether these benefits of cross-context propagation</span>
252 <span class="a">+outweigh any tensions created by applying the same license across different risk profiles. The synthesis</span>
253 <span class="a">+should conclude with a holistic assessment of whether the propagation serves the overarching goals</span>
254 <span class="a">+of the FOSS community.</span>

256 <span class="a">+4. Contextual Copyleft from Traditional Software to Generative AI</span>
257 <span class="a">+In this section, we evaluate the proposed CCIA license using the three-part analysis described in the</span>
258 <span class="a">+previous section. First, we examine the legal mechanisms through which copyleft licenses could extend</span>
259 <span class="a">+from training data to AI models, focusing on the critical question of whether AI training constitutes</span>
260 <span class="a">+fair use under US copyright law. This legal analysis proceeds under the assumption that such training</span>
261 <span class="a">+does not automatically qualify as fair use, allowing us to explore how licensing terms might apply.</span>
262 <span class="a">+</span>
263 <span class="a">+         Second, we evaluate the use of copyleft licenses in each context individually—traditional</span>
264 <span class="a">+software and generative AI—to understand how the justification for these licenses changes across</span>
265 <span class="a">+contexts. This comparative analysis reveals significant differences in the risk profiles between</span>
266 <span class="a">+traditional open source software and open source AI, particularly regarding the potential for direct</span>
267 <span class="a">+misuse and harm generation.</span>
268 <span class="a">+</span>
269 <span class="a">+         Finally, we synthesize these analyses to demonstrate that a copyleft license that propagates to</span>
270 <span class="a">+AI systems offers three key benefits: it grants developers greater agency over how their code is used</span>
271 <span class="a">+in AI development, stimulates the creation of open source generative AI systems, and combats “open-</span>
272 <span class="a">+washing” practices. Practical enforcement of the CCAI license against AI models trained on publicly</span>
273 <span class="a">+available data might be challenging due to difficulties in detecting unauthorized usage. However, even</span>
274 <span class="a">+in cases where enforcement is challenging, the existence of clearly articulated license terms can serve</span>
275 <span class="a">+as an effective deterrent against non-compliant use by law-abiding entities. We also acknowledge that</span>
276 <span class="a">+the increased risks associated with open source AI, particularly the potential for direct misuse, require</span>
277 <span class="a">+consideration of complementary regulatory approaches. We conclude by arguing that responsible AI</span>
278 <span class="a">+regulation can work alongside copyleft licensing to better align the risks and benefits of free software</span>
279 <span class="a">+principles in the context of generative AI.</span>

283 4.1. Legal Analysis
284 <span class="a">+Consider the case of a developer trying to prevent their publicly published work from being used to</span>
285 <span class="a">+train generative AI models. What legal tools are available to this person, and under what circumstances</span>
286 <span class="a">+can they be used?</span>
287 <span class="a">+</span>
288 <span class="a">+         Under United States (US) copyright law, authors of original works receive several exclusive</span>
289 <span class="a">+rights, codified in 17 U.S.C. § 106. These include the rights to reproduce the work, prepare derivative</span>
290 <span class="a">+works based on it, distribute copies to the public, and publicly perform or display the work. However,</span>
291 <span class="a">+these rights are subject to statutory limitations, including exceptions such as fair use and compulsory</span>
292 <span class="a">+licensing schemes that serve broader social and economic interests. Copyright automatically applies to</span>
293 <span class="a">+original works when they are fixed in a tangible medium, including digital formats such as online</span>
294 <span class="a">+content. This means that any original code published online is immediately protected. Licenses specify</span>
295 <span class="a">+the rights that are legally reserved for the copyright holder and grant other rights to users of the work,</span>
296 <span class="a">+often with attached terms and conditions. In the case of copyleft, this arrangement is reversed via a</span>
297 <span class="a">+license, with all rights granted to the user on the condition that derivative works are released under</span>
298 <span class="a">+the same license.</span>
299 <span class="a">+</span>
300 <span class="a">+         Fair use is the primary limitation of licenses serving as a method to grant developers greater</span>
301 <span class="a">+control over how their code is used. Licenses, as contracts between the copyright owner and the</span>
302 <span class="a">+software user, can only place terms on rights reserved for the copyright holder. Under US copyright</span>
303 <span class="a">+law, the doctrine of fair use, determined by a set of four standards, allows an existing work to be used</span>
304 <span class="a">+to create a new work such that it is no longer considered a derivative work of the original if the new</span>
305 <span class="a">+work is sufficiently “transformative.” Therefore, the original copyright holder cannot impose</span>
306 <span class="a">+conditions on how their work is used if the new work qualifies as fair use.</span>
307 <span class="a">+</span>
308 <span class="a">+         Regarding generative AI, if it becomes established through the courts or legislation that using</span>
309 <span class="a">+code to train a model is always fair use of that training data, the discussion of licenses in this context</span>
310 <span class="a">+becomes irrelevant. Licenses would be unable to restrict what models can train on, given that publicly</span>
311 <span class="a">+available code is unrestricted. However, there is uncertainty about whether training models on code</span>
312 <span class="a">+is, or should be, regarded as fair use (Charlesworth 2024). For instance, some argue that the</span>
313 <span class="a">+substantially similar relationship between the input and output of a model, the commercial purpose</span>
314 <span class="a">+of many AI, models, and how models can replace the source material in the market suggest that</span>
315 <span class="a">+training on copyrighted material may not qualify as fair use (Shen 2024).</span>
316 <span class="a">+</span>
317 <span class="a">+         The May 2025 US Copyright Office report provides crucial (although non-binding) guidance</span>
318 <span class="a">+on this uncertainty. The report concludes that training AI models on copyrighted works “clearly</span>
319 <span class="a">+</span>
320 <span class="a">+                                                                                                                    8</span>
321 <span class="a">+</span>
322 implicates the right of reproduction,” explicitly rejecting any blanket fair use protection for AI training
323 <span class="a">+(US Copyright Oﬀice 2025). The Office emphasized that the reproduction right is triggered when</span>
324 <span class="a">+copyrighted works are copied into training datasets, regardless of whether those copies are retained</span>
325 <span class="a">+after the training process is complete. This finding directly challenges the argument that AI training</span>
326 <span class="a">+constitutes non-expressive use, exempt from copyright concerns. The report further notes that</span>
327 <span class="a">+commercial uses creating competing content are less likely to qualify as fair use, while research and</span>
328 <span class="a">+non-commercial uses receive more favorable treatment—a distinction particularly relevant for</span>
329 <span class="a">+commercial code generation models.</span>
330 <span class="a">+</span>
331 <span class="a">+         With the assumption that this sort of training is not fair use, we can hypothesize that</span>
332 <span class="a">+developers can use licenses to restrict how their code is used to train generative AI models. In this</span>
333 <span class="a">+case, generative AI models trained on code could plausibly be considered derivative works of the</span>
334 <span class="a">+training data, although this interpretation remains legally unsettled, as the exact code from the training</span>
335 <span class="a">+data is not present in the model’s codebase and is never technically executed by the model. A similar</span>
336 <span class="a">+conclusion as to the ability of open source licenses to propagate to AI models has been found where</span>
337 <span class="a">+Benhamou argues “when the AI model incorporates open code into its program or open data into its</span>
338 <span class="a">+training dataset, there is a risk of propagation, i.e. a risk that the entire model or the input data become</span>
339 <span class="a">+fully open, as the AI model or the input can be considered as derivative of the open code or data,</span>
340 <span class="a">+whose license requires derivatives to remain open” (Benhamou 2024).</span>
341 <span class="a">+</span>
342 <span class="a">+         The uncertainty surrounding whether the use of open source code as training data constitutes</span>
343 <span class="a">+fair use warrants consideration in its own right. The current confusion in the legal landscape naturally</span>
344 <span class="a">+leads developers to restrict licenses defensively, potentially hindering both AI innovation and open</span>
345 <span class="a">+source collaboration. A clear, purpose-built license can overcome this confusion by providing explicit</span>
346 <span class="a">+guidance that operates effectively under multiple legal scenarios. If training is not deemed fair use,</span>
347 <span class="a">+then licenses become the primary mechanism for controlling the use of AI training. A license</span>
348 <span class="a">+specifically addressing AI training for code generation models would provide clear guidance to all</span>
349 <span class="a">+parties, potentially increasing the pool of code available for training while respecting developer</span>
350 <span class="a">+preferences. However, if courts ultimately determine that training AI models constitutes fair use, such</span>
351 <span class="a">+licensing terms would have no legal force. And it is worth noting that jurisdictions outside the US,</span>
352 <span class="a">+such as the EU, provide explicit text and data mining (TDM) exceptions, which permit data mining</span>
353 <span class="a">+unless right-holders explicitly opt-out through licensing terms. Thus, the CCAI license terms could</span>
354 <span class="a">+effectively serve as an explicit opt-out of such statutory exceptions under EU law. Therefore, we see</span>
355 <span class="a">+</span>
356 <span class="a">+                                                                                                                    9</span>
357 <span class="a">+</span>
358 legal value in such a license, regardless of how the US courts ultimately rule on the question of fair
359 <span class="a">+use.</span>

361 <span class="a">+4.2. Policy Analysis in Each Context</span>
362 <span class="a">+The FOSS community generally agrees on the benefits of FOSS, which, according to the community,</span>
363 <span class="a">+include reliability, security, and low cost (AlMarzouq et al. 2005). However, sub-movements of the</span>
364 <span class="a">+community disagree on the intellectual justification for FOSS. The free software (FS) movement</span>
365 <span class="a">+presents moral claims to advocate for free software as defined above. Then, there is the open source</span>
366 <span class="a">+(OS) movement, which offers pragmatic arguments regarding the value of free software. The</span>
367 <span class="a">+differences can be summed up as “open source is a development methodology; free software is a</span>
368 <span class="a">+social movement,” but their “practical recommendations” are largely similar (Stallman 2002).</span>
369 <span class="a">+</span>
370 <span class="a">+         The FS movement argues for free software based on what Richard Stallman describes as the</span>
371 <span class="a">+“Golden Rule” applied to software development. He argues that proprietary software is destructive</span>
372 <span class="a">+insofar as it “reduces the amount and the ways that a program can be used” and, as a result, reduces</span>
373 <span class="a">+the wealth humanity derives from the program (Stallman 2002). He ties this to Kantian ethics, arguing</span>
374 <span class="a">+that a good citizen does not use destructive means, such as proprietary software, to become wealthier.</span>
375 <span class="a">+With this, he concludes that “programmers have the duty to encourage others to share, redistribute,</span>
376 <span class="a">+study, and improve the software we write” (Stallman 2002). Another way that this has been formulated</span>
377 <span class="a">+is to start from the premise that software has permeated society, and, under a proprietary model,</span>
378 <span class="a">+software producers can control the availability of and access to software. As a result, “we are beholden</span>
379 <span class="a">+to these producers to run our modern society and are theoretically at their whim” (Heyns 2012).</span>
380 <span class="a">+Proponents of FS argue that this outcome is undesirable. Instead, software should be regarded as</span>
381 <span class="a">+infrastructure, as it is essential for societal participation and engagement. Thus, the assertion is made</span>
382 <span class="a">+that society should regulate access to the software “infrastructure” rather than software companies.</span>
383 <span class="a">+This approach helps prevent what has been termed “the digital divide,” wherein select groups of</span>
384 <span class="a">+people have access to the broad digital ecosystem (James 2003).</span>
385 <span class="a">+</span>
386 <span class="a">+         The OS movement advocates for free software from a strictly pragmatic or utilitarian</span>
387 <span class="a">+framework, arguing that free software is more beneficial to society than proprietary software. Software</span>
388 <span class="a">+plays a critical role today, making society interested in the quality and reliability of the software that is</span>
389 <span class="a">+produced and used. OS advocates argue that making source code freely available online for public</span>
390 <span class="a">+collaboration results in high-quality and highly reliable software. The OS community generally posits</span>
391 <span class="a">+that this is due to the ability to peer review software and provide more timely fixes because more</span>
392 <span class="a">+</span>
393 <span class="a">+                                                                                                                   10</span>
394 <span class="a">+</span>
395 developers can review source code in search of bugs and security risks. When these issues are
396 <span class="a">+identified, a distributed community can work together to create solutions, rather than a select group</span>
397 <span class="a">+within a specific company addressing the problem. The idea that a distributed community can lead to</span>
398 <span class="a">+better software outcomes is summed up in the famous Linus’s Law: “Given enough eyeballs, all bugs</span>
399 <span class="a">+are shallow” (Raymond 1999). OS is distinct from FS in that it does not make moral claims, but is</span>
400 <span class="a">+based on pragmatic claims regarding which development model will yield the highest quality and most</span>
401 <span class="a">+reliable software.</span>
402 <span class="a">+</span>
403 <span class="a">+         The primary risks of releasing certain pieces of traditional software as open source include</span>
404 <span class="a">+security vulnerabilities and software misuse (Zajdel, Costa, and Mili 2022). Regarding security</span>
405 <span class="a">+vulnerabilities, the risk of malicious actors discovering and exploiting these vulnerabilities can be</span>
406 <span class="a">+mitigated by allowing multiple developers to review the code and identify and fix bugs. Thus, “open</span>
407 <span class="a">+source does not pose any significant barriers to security but rather reinforces sound security practices</span>
408 <span class="a">+by involving many people that expose bugs quickly and offers side-effects that provide customers and</span>
409 <span class="a">+the community with concrete examples of reusable, secure, and working code” (Clarke, Dorwin, and</span>
410 <span class="a">+Nash 2009).</span>
411 <span class="a">+</span>
412 <span class="a">+         Moving from the traditional software context to generative AI, the risk profile changes. The</span>
413 <span class="a">+security risks of open source AI are non-negligible, with a survey revealing that 10% of IT decision-</span>
414 <span class="a">+makers have accidentally installed malicious code in their organizations through the use of open source</span>
415 <span class="a">+AI tools.5 Furthermore, the risk of actors using the code for malicious purposes is a concern that is</span>
416 <span class="a">+less directly countered by the benefits of open source software and is more challenging to mitigate.</span>
417 <span class="a">+Once open source software is out in the world, there is by definition no way that the developers can</span>
418 <span class="a">+control its use; while Meta’s Llama terms of service ostensibly prohibit its use for military purposes,</span>
419 <span class="a">+researchers associated with the Chinese People’s Liberation Army were still able to use it to create a</span>
420 <span class="a">+tool to gather and synthesize intelligence (Wilson and Hine 2025).</span>
421 <span class="a">+</span>
422 <span class="a">+         Additional risks associated with open source AI include misuse, where software is used in a</span>
423 <span class="a">+technically unintended manner to inflict harm. When it comes to OSS related to generative AI, there</span>
424 <span class="a">+are several pathways to explicit harm, including the exploitation of security vulnerabilities and the</span>
425 <span class="a">+ability to create harmful content, particularly given the challenges of rolling back or updating models</span>
426 <span class="a">+(Eiras, Petrov, Vidgen, Witt, et al. 2024). The possibility of generating harmful content or using a</span>
427 <span class="a">+foundation model for malicious activities is particularly concerning given the nature of such misuse.</span>
428 <span class="a">+</span>
429 <span class="a">+5 https://www.anaconda.com/lp/state-of-enterprise-open-source-ai</span>
430 <span class="a">+</span>
431 <span class="a">+                                                                                                                   11</span>
432 <span class="a">+</span>
433 This type of misuse is primarily direct, contrasting with actor-dependent misuse seen in traditional
434 <span class="a">+software. For example, if a foundation model is fine-tuned on a company’s emails, it may generate</span>
435 <span class="a">+highly effective phishing emails, posing a more significant threat to unsuspecting employees. This</span>
436 <span class="a">+scenario differs fundamentally from using an open source email client to send phishing emails. Here,</span>
437 <span class="a">+the open source technology in the case of generative AI is being used to amplify harm. In contrast, in</span>
438 <span class="a">+the traditional software case, the open source software operates as intended but is misused by the</span>
439 <span class="a">+actor to inflict damage. The risk of this explicit misuse in generative AI is significantly higher compared</span>
440 <span class="a">+to traditional software.</span>

442 <span class="a">+4.3 Synthesizing the Analysis</span>
443 <span class="a">+The legal analysis has shown that the CCAI license can propagate from training data to a generative</span>
444 <span class="a">+AI model itself, while the policy analysis has shown strong support for the open-sourcing of traditional</span>
445 <span class="a">+software, highlighting both substantial benefits and risks in the context of open source AI. The</span>
446 <span class="a">+particular risk of direct misuse stands out as a significant concern, particularly in the context of open</span>
447 <span class="a">+source AI, compared to traditional software, which could complicate the justification for open source</span>
448 <span class="a">+approaches in the generative AI context. In this section, we will take these insights and show that the</span>
449 <span class="a">+CCAI license could benefit both the open source and AI development communities when used in</span>
450 <span class="a">+particular regulatory schemes. The promise such a license holds for both communities is first argued,</span>
451 <span class="a">+and then a discussion of regulatory schemes follows.</span>
452 <span class="a">+</span>
453 <span class="a">+         This type of copyleft license offers three key advantages. First, it would give developers</span>
454 <span class="a">+enhanced control over how their code is incorporated into generative AI development. Second, it</span>
455 <span class="a">+would promote the development of genuinely open source generative AI systems. Third, it would help</span>
456 <span class="a">+combat “open-washing” practices that dilute the real benefits that open source generative AI systems</span>
457 <span class="a">+can provide.</span>
458 <span class="a">+</span>
459 <span class="a">+         First, a copyleft license that propagates from traditional software to generative AI systems</span>
460 <span class="a">+provides an important mechanism to give developers greater control over how their code is used in</span>
461 <span class="a">+AI systems. Today, when developers open source their code, they lose control over what types of</span>
462 <span class="a">+generative AI models are trained on their code and for what purposes those AI models are employed.</span>
463 <span class="a">+On the one hand, this is a feature, not a bug. Open source licenses are explicitly designed to allow the</span>
464 <span class="a">+unrestricted use of code, enabling others to use the original work in new and innovative ways. At the</span>
465 <span class="a">+same time, developers often do not want their work to contribute to nefarious ends, which could</span>
466 <span class="a">+occur if their code is used to train a generative AI model intended to cause harm, for example. This</span>
467 <span class="a">+</span>
468 <span class="a">+                                                                                                                   12</span>
469 <span class="a">+</span>
470 has led some developers to add “no AI” clauses to existing licenses to prohibit using their code for
471 <span class="a">+training an AI model.6</span>
472 <span class="a">+</span>
473 <span class="a">+         The tension within the open source community regarding the balance between freely sharing</span>
474 <span class="a">+software and caution towards unrestricted training on such software is evident in the backlash against</span>
475 <span class="a">+the release of CoPilot. GitHub CoPilot is an AI model developed specifically to generate code and,</span>
476 <span class="a">+consequently, requires a vast amount of code for its training data. This prompted GitHub to use code</span>
477 <span class="a">+from numerous open source repositories, asserting that the training of their model constituted fair use</span>
478 <span class="a">+and, therefore, the licensing terms of the code did not apply. Many in the FOSS community disagreed,</span>
479 <span class="a">+leading to a boycott of GitHub by many open source developers and a class-action lawsuit against</span>
480 <span class="a">+GitHub, Microsoft, OpenAI, and others involved in creating CoPilot (Class Action Complaint 3:22-</span>
481 <span class="a">+cv-06823). Some even argue that foundational models like CoPilot may discourage developers from</span>
482 <span class="a">+contributing to open source projects, as these models “exploit the principle of solidarity operating</span>
483 <span class="a">+within the movement” and have the potential to undermine the movement altogether (Novobilská</span>
484 <span class="a">+2023). An open source license with a copyleft clause would specify that it propagates from training</span>
485 <span class="a">+data to generative AI models, providing open source developers a middle ground between allowing</span>
486 <span class="a">+unconstrained training and limiting innovation and openness by restricting all training.</span>
487 <span class="a">+</span>
488 <span class="a">+         Second, introducing and adopting a copyleft license that propagates from training data to AI</span>
489 <span class="a">+models would lead to more open source generative AI models—an outcome that the open source</span>
490 <span class="a">+community supports.7 It is well known that training generative AI models requires vast amounts of</span>
491 <span class="a">+data, so a model designed to generate code (such as GitHub’s CoPilot) would need substantial code</span>
492 <span class="a">+to train on. Open source repositories are currently the solution for model developers. Suppose such a</span>
493 <span class="a">+license were widely adopted within the FOSS community. Of course, the effectiveness of the CCAI</span>
494 <span class="a">+license depends heavily on its widespread adoption. If only a small subset of developers adopts it,</span>
495 <span class="a">+proprietary AI developers may simply avoid CCAI-licensed code. A license of this kind would outline</span>
496 <span class="a">+which portion of the open source code online is available for use by open source models during</span>
497 <span class="a">+training. This would create an incentive for the open sourcing of new generative AI models that</span>
498 <span class="a">+produce code, as it provides open source model developers with access to training data that proprietary</span>
499 <span class="a">+models cannot use. The incentivization and creation of open source generative AI models offer new</span>
500 <span class="a">+opportunities for external oversight, technical innovation, and decentralized control over AI</span>
501 <span class="a">+development compared to closed-source models (Seger et al. 2023).</span>
502 <span class="a">+</span>
503 <span class="a">+6 https://github.com/non-ai-licenses/non-ai-licenses</span>
504 <span class="a">+7 https://opensource.org/ai/open-source-ai-deﬁnition</span>
505 <span class="a">+</span>
506 <span class="a">+                                                                                                                   13</span>
507 <span class="a">+</span>
508          Third, a copyleft license of this type could provide more explicit, enforceable definitions for
509 <span class="a">+open source AI, helping to combat “open-washing.” Open-washing refers to AI models that claim to</span>
510 <span class="a">+be open source by publicly sharing only one component, such as the model weights (Liesenfeld and</span>
511 <span class="a">+Dingemanse 2024). While this may allow users to download and run the model on their computers, it</span>
512 <span class="a">+fails to convey the comprehensive understanding of software freedom that the open source</span>
513 <span class="a">+community has cultivated; crucial contexts, including the code that trained the model and details of</span>
514 <span class="a">+the training data, are absent. The current language in copyleft licenses mandates that the “entire work”</span>
515 <span class="a">+be shared under the same license as the original, not just the portion of the software copied from the</span>
516 <span class="a">+original work. From a model developer’s perspective, the “entire work” could encompass not only the</span>
517 <span class="a">+model weights but also the underlying model architecture, supporting code, and details about the</span>
518 <span class="a">+training data. The interpretation of “entire work” as it applies to generative AI could broaden the</span>
519 <span class="a">+required components of an AI system that must be open to tackle the open-washing observed in the</span>
520 <span class="a">+AI development community effectively.</span>
521 <span class="a">+</span>
522 <span class="a">+         These three advantages of the CCAI license must be considered in light of the above legal and</span>
523 <span class="a">+policy analysis. Specifically, the risk of direct misuse of open source AI is that it is used for malicious</span>
524 <span class="a">+purposes. The challenge is that open source licenses must not restrict how a technology can be used,</span>
525 <span class="a">+as defined by the principles of open source.8 However, while it can’t be prohibited, there is precedent</span>
526 <span class="a">+within the FOSS community resisting the misuse of open source software. For instance, a former</span>
527 <span class="a">+developer at Chef, a computer management software company, created open source tools that helped</span>
528 <span class="a">+customers work with Chef’s software. After leaving the company, he discovered a contract between</span>
529 <span class="a">+Chef and the US Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE), prompting him to remove the code</span>
530 <span class="a">+with a note stating, “I have a moral and ethical obligation to prevent my source [code] from being</span>
531 <span class="a">+used for evil.”9 This decision caused an outage for multiple customers, leading Chef to republish an</span>
532 <span class="a">+older version of the open source tool’s source code on the company’s corporate GitHub account, an</span>
533 <span class="a">+action permitted by the software freedom provisions of the original license. However, it should be</span>
534 <span class="a">+noted that the developer was able to protest Chef’s collaboration with ICE because he had authority</span>
535 <span class="a">+over that code.</span>
536 <span class="a">+</span>
537 <span class="a">+         One possible way to mitigate the risks of open source generative AI would be to introduce a</span>
538 <span class="a">+responsible AI use regulation that limits how open source models can be used to cause direct harm.</span>
539 <span class="a">+For instance, part of the EU AI Act prevents AI systems from using subliminal, manipulative, or</span>
540 <span class="a">+</span>
541 <span class="a">+8 https://opensource.org/osd</span>
542 <span class="a">+9 https://www.zdnet.com/article/no-source-code-for-evil-developers-pressure-chef-software-to-cut-ties-with-ice/</span>
543 <span class="a">+</span>
544 <span class="a">+                                                                                                                   14</span>
545 <span class="a">+</span>
546 deceptive techniques that distort a person’s behavior and impair decision-making, causing significant
547 <span class="a">+harm, or exploiting individuals or groups based on age, disability, or socio-economic conditions to</span>
548 <span class="a">+influence behaviors in a harmful way (European Union 2024). Although these provisions have been</span>
549 <span class="a">+criticized for a lack of specificity (Montag and Finck 2024), similar restrictions in the US could help</span>
550 <span class="a">+mitigate the risk of misuse of open source generative AI systems. This would help rebalance the risks</span>
551 <span class="a">+and benefits of open source generative AI closer to those of traditional open source software, allowing</span>
552 <span class="a">+for a stronger endorsement of copyleft in both contexts.</span>

554 <span class="a">+5. Conclusion</span>
555 <span class="a">+The analysis presented in this article demonstrates that the Contextual Copyleft AI (CCAI) license</span>
556 <span class="a">+represents a promising approach to extending traditional copyleft principles into the domain of</span>
557 <span class="a">+generative AI development. Through our structured evaluation framework, we have determined that</span>
558 <span class="a">+such a license is legally feasible under current copyright law, assuming that AI training does not</span>
559 <span class="a">+constitute fair use, and offers substantial benefits to both the open source and AI development</span>
560 <span class="a">+communities. The license addresses three critical challenges facing the FOSS community in the age of</span>
561 <span class="a">+generative AI: providing developers with meaningful control over how their code is incorporated into</span>
562 <span class="a">+AI systems, creating incentives for genuinely open source AI development, and combating the dilution</span>
563 <span class="a">+of open source principles through open-washing practices. These advantages align with the</span>
564 <span class="a">+fundamental goals of the FOSS movement while adapting to the unique characteristics of generative</span>
565 <span class="a">+AI technologies.</span>
566 <span class="a">+</span>
567 <span class="a">+         However, our policy analysis reveals that the risk profile of open source generative AI differs</span>
568 <span class="a">+significantly from that of traditional software, particularly regarding the potential for direct misuse,</span>
569 <span class="a">+where AI systems can be employed to generate harmful content or amplify malicious activities. This</span>
570 <span class="a">+increased risk profile challenges the traditional cost-benefit calculus that has historically justified</span>
571 <span class="a">+copyleft licensing in software development. The analysis suggests that while the benefits of open</span>
572 <span class="a">+source AI remain substantial—including enhanced oversight, innovation, and decentralized control—</span>
573 <span class="a">+the heightened risks require careful consideration and potentially complementary regulatory</span>
574 <span class="a">+mechanisms to achieve an appropriate balance.</span>
575 <span class="a">+</span>
576 <span class="a">+         The path forward for implementing the CCAI license depends critically on the development</span>
577 <span class="a">+of comprehensive regulatory frameworks that address the responsible use of AI systems. Such</span>
578 <span class="a">+regulations, exemplified by provisions in the EU AI Act that restrict manipulative and exploitative AI</span>
579 <span class="a">+applications, could help mitigate the direct misuse risks that complicate the justification for open</span>
580 <span class="a">+</span>
581 <span class="a">+                                                                                                                   15</span>
582 <span class="a">+</span>
583 source AI. CCAI alone does not eliminate misuse risks—it ensures openness but cannot prevent
584 <span class="a">+malicious use of openly released models. Thus, its practical effectiveness depends significantly on</span>
585 <span class="a">+complementary regulatory measures addressing responsible use of AI. Within such a regulatory</span>
586 <span class="a">+environment, the CCAI license emerges as a valuable tool for preserving the core values of software</span>
587 <span class="a">+freedom while adapting to the realities of modern AI development. This analysis thus contributes to</span>
588 <span class="a">+the broader discourse on maintaining the integrity and relevance of FOSS principles as technology</span>
589 <span class="a">+continues to evolve, demonstrating that thoughtful adaptation of traditional licensing mechanisms can</span>
590 <span class="a">+help navigate the complex challenges posed by generative AI while upholding the fundamental</span>
591 <span class="a">+commitments of the open source community.</span>
