28 <a id="l19" href="#l19">19</a> ## Mechanism: retrieval is the active ingredient
29 <a id="l20" href="#l20">20</a> 
30 <a id="l21" href="#l21">21</a> Spacing is the schedule; retrieval is the drug. Spacing rereading barely beats massed rereading; the retention gain lives in forcing recall, then checking.
31 <a id="l22" href="#l22">22</a> 
32 <a id="l23" href="#l23">23</a> - Active recall first, feedback second: the act of pulling the answer out (not reading it) is what consolidates. A card where you peek before trying is a reread, not a review.
33 <a id="l24" href="#l24">24</a> - Testing effect size: retrieval beats rereading by ~2x at a one-week delay, and the gap widens as the delay grows (Roediger/Karpicke). The longer the gap, the more retrieval pays.
34 <a id="l25" href="#l25">25</a> - Desirable difficulty: a review you struggle slightly to recall consolidates more than one that is instant. A card always answered in &lt;1s is over-reviewed; one you always fail is under-learned or badly written.
35 <a id="l26" href="#l26">26</a> - Recognition vs recall: multiple-choice and &quot;looks familiar&quot; build familiarity, not retrievability. Build cards that demand production (type the answer, say it aloud), not yes/no recognition.
36 <a id="l27" href="#l27">27</a> - Recollection, not familiarity, is the goal: SR defeats the illusion of knowing (you reread, it feels known, you cannot produce it). The proof of learning is reproducing the answer unaided.
37 <a id="l28" href="#l28">28</a> - No retrieval, no spacing benefit: passive re-exposure on a spaced schedule (re-reading notes every few days) yields a fraction of the gain. If you cannot make yourself retrieve, the schedule is wasted.
38 <a id="l29" href="#l29">29</a> - Feedback must be honest: grade against the real answer before marking known. Self-flattery (&quot;I basically had it&quot;) is the silent killer; the curve punishes lenient grading within a week.
39 <a id="l30" href="#l30">30</a> 
40 <a id="l31" href="#l31">31</a> ## The forgetting curve and interval ladder
41 <a id="l32" href="#l32">32</a> 
42 <a id="l33" href="#l33">33</a> - Curve shape: steepest in the first 24h. Nonsense-syllable retention drops to ~33% within a day and ~25% within a month (Ebbinghaus); real meaningful material decays slower, but the early-steep shape holds.
43 <a id="l34" href="#l34">34</a> - The first review is the highest-leverage: skipping day-1 review loses the most. The schedule front-loads reviews because that is where decay is fastest.
44 <a id="l35" href="#l35">35</a> - Expanding beats equal spacing for a fixed review budget: 1, 3, 7, 14, 28 beats 5, 5, 5, 5, 5. Expanding catches early decay, then spends reviews where decay has slowed.
45 <a id="l36" href="#l36">36</a> - A typical first ladder (SM-2-ish): 1d -&gt; 3d -&gt; 7d -&gt; 14d -&gt; 28d -&gt; 60d -&gt; 120d. Not a law; the algorithm adjusts the next interval from your grade.
46 <a id="l37" href="#l37">37</a> - The interval is a prediction of when you are about to forget, not a target to hit. Shorter-than-expected means you forgot, the interval was too long. Longer-than-expected and still easy means intervals are too short.
47 <a id="l38" href="#l38">38</a> - &quot;About to forget&quot; is the review moment: reviewing when recall is effortful but successful consolidates best. Reviewing when recall is instant wastes a slot; reviewing after you have forgotten relearns, does not reinforce.
48 <a id="l39" href="#l39">39</a> - Do not pre-empt the schedule: reviewing early &quot;to be safe&quot; collapses intervals and multiplies load. Trust the algorithm; if retention is off, fix the target or the card, not the timing.

50 <a id="l41" href="#l41">41</a> ## Algorithms: SM-2 vs FSRS
51 <a id="l42" href="#l42">42</a> 
52 <a id="l43" href="#l43">43</a> - SM-2 (Anki&#x27;s legacy engine, from SuperMemo, 1987): each grade multiplies the interval by an ease factor; ease drifts up on &quot;easy&quot;, down on &quot;hard&quot;. No target retention; the schedule is a heuristic.
53 <a id="l44" href="#l44">44</a> - SM-2 weakness: ease inflates on early easy cards then never recovers, producing absurd intervals (a card &quot;known&quot; after three easy taps lands at a 6-month interval you will actually fail). Interval explosion is the tell.
54 <a id="l45" href="#l45">45</a> - FSRS (Free Spaced Repetition Scheduler): optimizes to a desired retention (default 0.9) via 19 parameters fit to your personal review history. Replaces SM-2 in Anki as of ~2024; strictly better once you have ~1k reviews logged to fit on.
55 <a id="l46" href="#l46">46</a> - FSRS needs history: on a fresh profile with &lt;1000 reviews it falls back to defaults; the fit improves as data accumulates. Do not judge FSRS on the first week.
56 <a id="l47" href="#l47">47</a> - Set desired retention to 0.9 unless you have a reason. 0.85 = fewer reviews, more forgetting; 0.95+ = review load balloons. 0.97 costs ~2x the reviews of 0.90 (FSRS).
57 <a id="l48" href="#l48">48</a> - Retention target is the lever, not the interval: decide how much you can afford to forget, set the target, let the algorithm produce the intervals. Hand-tuning intervals per card is the #1000&#x27;s rabbit hole.
58 <a id="l49" href="#l49">49</a> - Re-grade on actual recall, not difficulty feeling: marking &quot;hard&quot; because you disliked the question inflates load. Mark hard only when recall was slow or partial, not when the card annoys you.
59 <a id="l50" href="#l50">50</a> - Migration: SM-2 -&gt; FSRS re-estimates intervals from history; expect some cards to jump shorter (they were over-scheduled) and some longer (under-scheduled). Let it settle a month before judging.

61 <a id="l52" href="#l52">52</a> ## Card design
62 <a id="l53" href="#l53">53</a> 
63 <a id="l54" href="#l54">54</a> - One atomic fact per card: a card with three things to recall fails as one. If you miss one, you fail all three and cannot tell which. Split into three cards; review cost is the same, signal is clean.
64 <a id="l55" href="#l55">55</a> - Minimum information: the question should be answerable in a word or short phrase. A paragraph on the answer side means you are not testing recall, you are re-reading.
65 <a id="l56" href="#l56">56</a> - Cloze (fill-the-blank) for facts inside a sentence; Q&amp;A for concepts needing a produced answer. Cloze is faster to author and good for lists and definitions; Q&amp;A for anything where the blank gives away the answer.
66 <a id="l57" href="#l57">57</a> - Context-sufficiency: the card must make sense without the deck around it. A card that reads &quot;the third one&quot; only works in its original list; six months later it is opaque. Write the full question on the card.
67 <a id="l58" href="#l58">58</a> - Avoid yes/no and &quot;did you understand&quot;: recognition, not recall. &quot;Is X true?&quot; lets familiarity pass. Force production: &quot;What is X?&quot;
68 <a id="l59" href="#l59">59</a> - Reverse cards for bidirectional facts: capital -&gt; country and country -&gt; capital are two cards. Language, names, and pairs need both directions or you build one-way recall.
69 <a id="l60" href="#l60">60</a> - Interference kills: near-synonyms (two foreign words for &quot;big&quot;), adjacent numbers (cranial nerves), similar formulas. If you keep confusing two cards, they interfere: merge, distinguish with a mnemonic, or stagger them apart.
70 <a id="l61" href="#l61">61</a> - Images where they earn it: anatomy, maps, diagrams. A labeled image beats a sentence for spatial and visual facts. Do not decorate; an image earns its place only if recall depends on it.
71 <a id="l62" href="#l62">62</a> - Do not copy textbook paragraphs as cards: they fail the atomic test and you will suspend them within a month. Pre-process: read, understand, then write 5-10 atomic cards capturing what you would want to recall in a year.
72 <a id="l63" href="#l63">63</a> - Source on the card: tag with origin so you can fix it when it is wrong. A card with no source cannot be audited; six months later you will not know if you misremembered the fact or the source did.
73 <a id="l64" href="#l64">64</a> 
74 <a id="l65" href="#l65">65</a> ## Review economy and target retention
75 <a id="l66" href="#l66">66</a> 
76 <a id="l67" href="#l67">67</a> - The marginal-retention curve is steep above 0.9: each 1% of desired retention above 0.90 adds ~10% more reviews (FSRS). 0.97 is roughly double the load of 0.90 for ~7% more remembered.
77 <a id="l68" href="#l68">68</a> - Sustainable new-card rate: 10-20 new/day for a serious learner; each new card generates ~7-10 reviews over its first month. 50 new/day buries you within weeks.
78 <a id="l69" href="#l69">69</a> - The steady state: at 0.9 retention with steady additions, daily reviews settle around 7-10x your daily new-card count. 20 new/day -&gt; ~140-200 reviews/day at equilibrium.
79 <a id="l70" href="#l70">70</a> - Backlog is the failure mode: missing days compounds. A 3-day miss on a 150/day habit = 450 backlogged, which you will rush-grade (lenient, useless) or abandon. Daily, even if short, beats binge.
80 <a id="l71" href="#l71">71</a> - Cap reviews if you must, never new cards: cutting new cards keeps the deck from growing; cutting reviews lets the backlog rot. If overloaded, freeze new cards and grind reviews to zero first.
81 <a id="l72" href="#l72">72</a> - Review time budget: a mature card takes ~5-8s; a new or failed card 15-30s. 100 mature reviews ~10-15 min; 100 new or failing ~30-40 min. Budget by composition, not count.
82 <a id="l73" href="#l73">73</a> - Grade honestly even when slow: a review you got right-but-slow is &quot;hard&quot;, not &quot;good&quot;. Lenient grading under time pressure is the main reason decks silently rot.

84 <a id="l75" href="#l75">75</a> ## Leeches, ease hell, and failure modes
85 <a id="l76" href="#l76">76</a> 
86 <a id="l77" href="#l77">77</a> - Leech: a card you fail repeatedly. Anki flags at 8 lapses by default. A leech means the card is broken, not that you are dumb; fix or kill it.
87 <a id="l78" href="#l78">78</a> - Leech fixes, in order: rewrite it smaller, add a mnemonic, add an image, split it. If it still leeches after a rewrite, suspend it: one bad card drains disproportionate time.
88 <a id="l79" href="#l79">79</a> - Ease hell (SM-2): ease factor driven to its 1.3 floor by repeated &quot;hard&quot; grades, so intervals barely grow and the card reviews daily forever. Symptom: a card you have seen 30 times still at a 1-day interval.
89 <a id="l80" href="#l80">80</a> - Ease hell fix: reset the ease (Anki: set due date + reset, or migrate to FSRS which has no ease floor). The root cause is usually a badly-written card or chronic lenient-then-panicked grading; fix the card.
90 <a id="l81" href="#l81">81</a> - The lenient-grade trap: marking &quot;good&quot; on a slow recall to keep the deck moving inflates intervals, then you fail the card a week later, hammer ease, enter hell. Grade by recall quality, not by desired speed.
91 <a id="l82" href="#l82">82</a> - Over-learning a failed card: re-learning it 5x the same day does not help retention; it trains short-term memory. One clean recall, then let the schedule carry it.
92 <a id="l83" href="#l83">83</a> - Deck bloat: pre-made decks of 10k cards feel like progress; the math says you will never review them all. The #1 adds cards they have already engaged with, not decks to &quot;cover later&quot;.
93 <a id="l84" href="#l84">84</a> - Suspended-card debt: suspending instead of deleting accumulates a graveyard you will never revisit. Periodically delete, not just suspend.

95 <a id="l86" href="#l86">86</a> ## When spaced repetition is the wrong tool
96 <a id="l87" href="#l87">87</a> 
97 <a id="l88" href="#l88">88</a> - Physical skills (serving a tennis ball, intubation): SR schedules recall of facts, not motor patterns. Motor learning needs massed, varied, feedback-driven practice, not spaced recall.
98 <a id="l89" href="#l89">89</a> - Procedures you use daily: if you do it every day, the job is the review; adding SR cards for it is overhead. SR is for things you would otherwise forget.
99 <a id="l90" href="#l90">90</a> - Understanding vs facts: SR holds facts and definitions; it does not build conceptual understanding. Learn the concept first (problems, explanation), then SR the facts you must recall cold.
100 <a id="l91" href="#l91">91</a> - Anything you can look up faster than recall: a rarely-used API endpoint you will always have docs for is not worth a card. SR costs review time; spend it on what must be in your head.
101 <a id="l92" href="#l92">92</a> - Near-term, then discard: material for a one-off task (a client&#x27;s internal jargon for a 2-week project) does not deserve lifelong retention. Use it, drop it.
102 <a id="l93" href="#l93">93</a> - Massive, rapidly-changing sets: a 5000-card med-school deck you will relearn on rotation is borderline; maintenance cost can exceed value. Prefer fewer, high-yield cards.
103 <a id="l94" href="#l94">94</a> 
104 <a id="l95" href="#l95">95</a> ## Habit and daily load
105 <a id="l96" href="#l96">96</a> 
106 <a id="l97" href="#l97">97</a> - Daily, not &quot;when I have time&quot;: the schedule assumes consistency. Two days on, five off collapses intervals and spikes load; the algorithm cannot fit your forgetting.
107 <a id="l98" href="#l98">98</a> - One deck, one habit: reviewing across 8 small decks in scattered sessions erodes the habit. Consolidate into a daily review you do at a fixed time.
108 <a id="l99" href="#l99">99</a> - Review before adding: new cards compete for today&#x27;s time with due reviews. Clear due first, then add new within your budget.
109 <a id="l100" href="#l100">100</a> - Mobile for reviews, desktop for adding: the friction of card creation belongs at a desk where you think; review is a 5-second-per-card loop you do anywhere.
110 <a id="l101" href="#l101">101</a> - Set a daily new-card ceiling and hold it for months: the temptation after a motivated weekend is to add 100; that is borrowing reviews from future-you at interest.
111 <a id="l102" href="#l102">102</a> - Audit the deck quarterly: delete leeches, rewrite confusing cards, check retention stats. A deck that is never audited decays toward a pile of cards you fail and ignore.
112 <a id="l103" href="#l103">103</a> - Do not review when exhausted: a tired review is a lenient review. Better to halve the load than to speed-grade 300 cards into uselessness.

114 <a id="l105" href="#l105">105</a> ## Situations
115 <a id="l106" href="#l106">106</a> 
116 <a id="l107" href="#l107">107</a> | Situation | Play |
117 <a id="l108" href="#l108">108</a> |---|---|
118 <a id="l109" href="#l109">109</a> | 500+ reviews backlogged | Freeze new cards, set a review cap you will actually do daily, grind to zero over 1-2 weeks. Do not binge-grade. |
119 <a id="l110" href="#l110">110</a> | Failed the same card 4 times today | It is a leech in the making. Rewrite it smaller or split it; if still failing, suspend. |
120 <a id="l111" href="#l111">111</a> | Learning a language with 50 near-synonyms | They will interfere. Add a distinguishing context to each card, or batch one synonym at a time over weeks. |
121 <a id="l112" href="#l112">112</a> | Exam in 3 days | SR will not save you. Massed practice plus practice questions now; feed what stuck into SR after. |
122 <a id="l113" href="#l113">113</a> | A card whose answer is a paragraph | It is not a card, it is a note. Break into 5-8 atomic cards or delete it. |
123 <a id="l114" href="#l114">114</a> | Reviews take &gt;45 min/day | Lower desired retention to 0.85, cut new cards, or accept it. Load is a function of deck size and target. |
124 <a id="l115" href="#l115">115</a> | Want to learn to intubate | Wrong tool. Massed, supervised motor practice with feedback; SR only for the facts around it. |
125 <a id="l116" href="#l116">116</a> | Switching SM-2 to FSRS | Enable FSRS, let it optimize on existing history, keep retention at 0.9, wait a month before judging intervals. |
126 <a id="l117" href="#l117">117</a> | Two cards you keep swapping answers to | Interference. Merge into one card, add a distinguishing mnemonic, or stagger them. |
127 <a id="l118" href="#l118">118</a> | Just added 80 new cards in a day | Stop. You borrowed ~600 future reviews at interest. Cap at 10-20/day for the next week to absorb. |

129 <a id="l120" href="#l120">120</a> ## Where camps disagree
130 <a id="l121" href="#l121">121</a> 
131 <a id="l122" href="#l122">122</a> - **SM-2 vs FSRS**: SM-2 is simple, universal, no fit needed; FSRS optimizes to a retention target and beats SM-2 on review economy once you have ~1k+ reviews to fit on. FSRS wins for anyone with history; SM-2 only wins on a brand-new profile or a tool that does not support FSRS.
132 <a id="l123" href="#l123">123</a> - **Cloze vs Q&amp;A**: cloze wins for speed and lists (definitions, facts inside sentences); Q&amp;A wins for concepts where the blank would cue the answer and for anything requiring a produced explanation. The line is whether the blank leaks the answer.
133 <a id="l124" href="#l124">124</a> - **Pre-made decks vs self-made**: pre-made wins for bounded, high-consensus material (a country-capital deck, a drug-name deck); self-made wins for anything conceptual, because authoring is part of learning. Pre-made decks fail when the learner never engaged with the material first.
134 <a id="l125" href="#l125">125</a> - **Cap daily reviews vs unlimited**: capping prevents burnout and the backlog death-spiral but truncates the schedule; unlimited stays on schedule but can overwhelm. Cap when load threatens the habit; uncap when you can sustain it daily.
135 <a id="l126" href="#l126">126</a> - **Recognition vs recall grading**: recognition (flip-and-nod) is fast and lenient, builds familiarity; recall (produce before flipping) is slower, builds retrievability. Recall wins for anything you must produce; recognition is acceptable only for things you must merely recognize.
